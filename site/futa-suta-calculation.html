Employer taxes

FUTA and SUTA calculation

Unemployment tax is paid by the employer, on a capped slice of each employee's wages.

Two unemployment taxes are paid by the employer: federal (FUTA) and state (SUTA, also called SUI). Both apply only to the first part of each employee's wages for the year, so they are heaviest early in the year and stop once an employee passes the wage base.

FUTA

6.0% of the first $7,000 of each employee's wages, before the credit. An employer who pays state unemployment tax on time normally takes a credit that brings the rate to 0.6%, which is $42 per employee per year. The engine uses year-to-date wages to stop FUTA once the $7,000 is reached.

SUTA

Each state sets its own wage base and its own rate for each employer. In the 2026 rules the wage base ranges from $7,000 in Arkansas to $78,200 in Washington. Where a state publishes one, the new-employer rate runs from 1% (Alaska) to 4.1% (New York).

Your rate, or the new-employer rate

An established employer's rate is assigned by the state from its experience and printed on a rate notice. Send it as employer.stateUnemploymentRate and the engine uses it. If you do not, the engine uses the state's new-employer rate and says so in the line's detail; where a state publishes no single new-employer rate, it produces no line rather than a guess.

Questions

What is the difference between FUTA and SUTA?

FUTA is the federal unemployment tax, 6.0% of the first $7,000 per employee before a credit. SUTA is the state unemployment tax, with a different wage base and rate in each state. Both are paid by the employer.

How is SUTA calculated?

The employer's assigned rate times the employee's wages up to the state wage base for the year. A new employer pays the state's new-employer rate until it has an experience-based rate.

Do employees pay unemployment tax?

In most states no, it is employer-paid. A few states also collect a small employee contribution for related programs, which the engine returns as its own line.
