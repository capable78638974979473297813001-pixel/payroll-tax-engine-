Nonresident employee state withholding

Working in a state you do not live in.

A nonresident employee earns wages in a state where they do not live. In general the work state may tax those wages, and the employer withholds. States differ on the details: some exempt short stays, some have reciprocity with neighbours so only the home state taxes, and some use their own nonresident rates. The engine carries each state's rule.

Reciprocal states

16 states list reciprocal partners in the engine's rule files. For an employee covered by an agreement, the work state withholds nothing when the employee gives the right exemption form; the home state taxes the wages instead.

A worked case

New York resident status changes which line appears: an employee who works in New York but lives in New Jersey is a nonresident for New York. Compare an employee who lives in the state with one who does not, with the same pay. Differences show in NY_SIT and in which city lines apply.

Day-count rules

Some states do not tax a nonresident until they have worked more than a set number of days there; the certificate has a field for days worked, which the engine reads. The employer or the employee supplies it.

Maryland as an example

Maryland nonresidents pay a special local rate instead of a county rate, shown in the Maryland county tax example. Other states use separate nonresident tables or a flat nonresident rate.

Questions

Does a nonresident pay state tax where they work?

Generally yes: the work state may tax wages earned there, and the employer withholds, unless a reciprocity agreement or a short-stay rule exempts the employee.

What is a reciprocity agreement?

An agreement between two states under which an employee who lives in one and works in the other pays income tax only to the home state, usually by giving the employer an exemption form.
